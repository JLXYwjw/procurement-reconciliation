Part 1 · Business Objective

1. Why this analysis

The State of California runs procurement for 200,533 purchase orders across 24,732 suppliers. Procurement teams routinely cannot see three things that drive cost and risk:

  • Where money concentrates — a few suppliers or departments swallowing most spend.
  • Which buys are uncontrolled — purchases routed through non-standard ("flexible") methods that deserve review.
  • Where unit prices look off — anomalies that may signal overpricing or data-entry errors.

One-sentence goal: identify the small number of high-impact levers (concentration, control, and reviewable purchases) that let procurement negotiate better, set controls, and protect taxpayer money.

Insight

Spend spikes hard in fiscal-year-end months — Mar 2013 alone = $23B, several × a normal month (Jun/Apr 2014-15 also peak). This is a classic year-end spending spike pattern, not steady procurement.

Action

Split large year-end payments across the fiscal year to dampen budget-execution volatility and reduce last-minute "use it or lose it" purchases; put a monthly spend-target KPI on the dashboard.

Insight

A single department — Health Care Services — is 66% of all spend. Whatever drives health-payer costs drives the entire state budget.

Action

Stand up a dedicated healthcare-payer procurement program: with two-thirds of spend in one place, even a 1% improvement here outweighs anything else by far.

Insight

The top-10 suppliers are 41.4% of all spend and every one is a healthcare-payer organization (Health Net, L.A. Care, Delta Dental, Blue Cross, IEHP, Molina…). Money is concentrated, not fragmented.

Run consolidated negotiations / volume rebates with these 10. A 2–3% price bring-down on this tier is ~$1.0–1.6B of annual leverage.

Part 4 · Modeling

4. Business-driven methods (principle · why · how)

4.1 Supplier segmentation with KMeans clustering

Principle. KMeans groups suppliers so those inside a cluster are similar and clusters differ, using 5 scaled features: total spend, order count, category breadth, max-line share, average line value.

Why (business-driven). Negotiation effort is scarce; the question is "which suppliers deserve a strategic relationship?" A spend threshold alone would miss one-off giants vs. steady core vendors.

How. StandardScaler → k=3 (core / regular / one-off) → n_init=10 for stability, seed fixed.

Insight

KMeans isolates 8 core suppliers = 35.4% of spend (all healthcare-payer orgs), a regular middle and a long one-off tail of 14k small vendors.

Action

Put the 8 core vendors on a managed strategic-account program and consolidate one-off tail buys into fewer contracts to capture volume discounts.

4.2 Unit-cost anomaly detection (MAD rule, goods only)

Principle. The Median Absolute Deviation measures spread around the median robustly (unlike mean ± σ it is not distorted by the outliers it hunts); a line flags when unit cost >3×MAD above its segment median.

Why. A first pass on total spend flagged huge but legitimate medical contracts (83% of spend) — indefensible. We restrict to goods with quantity ≥ 2 (comparable unit prices) and compare within a product segment.

How & honest caveat. 32,433 of 124,290 comparable goods lines flag, ~$731.7M — but the top flags are legitimate big buys (helicopters, police interceptors, fire apparatus). Conclusion: without a SKU-level / market-price baseline, these flags are a review queue, not a verdict.

Insight

The flagged "outliers" cluster at high quantity & high unit cost — large but legitimate vehicle/medical purchases. Statistical flags alone mislead here.

Action

Use this as a weekly human-review queue; add a like-for-like (SKU-level or market-price) baseline later to turn it into a true overpricing detector.

4.3 Control-class review list (business rule)

Purchases via flexible / non-standard methods are grouped into a process-review list: $6.36B across 76,129 lines (Statement of Qualifications $1.73B, NCB $1.30B, State Programs $0.95B, Emergency $0.59B, Fair & Reasonable $0.42B, …). Not presumed bad — the subset a reviewer should sample for compliance.

Insight

Competitive and legally-exempt methods carry most spend, but $6.36B flows through flexible methods that deserve process review.

Action

Sample-review the top-200 flexible-method purchases by spend; set a "spend under management" target to pull more buying into controlled channels.

Why no deep learning here. This is tabular, ~346k rows, unlabeled. ML clustering + robust statistics answer the questions; DL needs far larger / sequence-shaped data and adds no gain for a compliance story that must stay explainable to stakeholders.
Part 5 · Recommendations

5. Pulling it together: an actionable plan

High impact

1 · Consolidate the 8 core vendors

35.4% of spend sits with 8 healthcare-payer suppliers. Consolidated contracts + volume rebates; a 2–3% bring-down = $1.0–1.6B annual leverage.

High impact

2 · Review the $6.36B flexible-method spend

Sample the top flexible buys for compliance; flexible methods are legitimate but higher-risk. Catch off-contract drift without choking operations.

Control

3 · Set a managed-spend target + tame year-end spikes

Raise spend through competitive/framework channels; smooth the fiscal-year-end buying spikes with a monthly spend-target KPI.

Governance

4 · Stand up a unit-price review queue

Keep the flagged goods list as a weekly human-review queue; add a SKU/market-price baseline to make it a real overpricing detector.
